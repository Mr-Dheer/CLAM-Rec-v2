\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

We propose Pivot-Rec, a two-stage sequential recommendation framework that ranks
candidate items using two complementary signals: a collaborative recommender
trained on interaction data, and a frozen large language model that reads item
titles. Given a user's chronological interaction history and a set of candidate
items, Pivot-Rec scores every candidate with both signals and combines them
according to the candidate item's popularity.

Stage~1 (Section~\ref{sec:stage1}) aligns SASRec item embeddings~\cite{sasrec}
with Sentence-BERT text embeddings~\cite{reimers2019sentence}, producing joint
sequential--textual item representations. Stage~2 (Section~\ref{sec:stage2})
assembles a user representation, these item representations, and the candidate
titles into a prompt for a frozen OPT-6.7B~\cite{opt}, which scores each
candidate title by its likelihood.

The two signals are not interchangeable. Their relative reliability depends on
item popularity, and the point at which one overtakes the
other varies across datasets (Section~\ref{sec:crossover-method}). Pivot-Rec
exploits this with PopGate (Section~\ref{sec:popgate}), which weights each
candidate's collaborative and language-model score by that candidate's own
popularity.


\subsection{Collaborative Filtering Backbone}
\label{sec:cf}

The collaborative component of Pivot-Rec is SASRec~\cite{sasrec}, a self-attentive
sequential recommender that models user behaviour from interaction data alone.
Every item is assigned a learned $50$-dimensional embedding, and a user's history
is represented as the sequence of embeddings of the items that user has interacted
with, truncated or padded to a fixed window of the most recent interactions and
combined with learned positional embeddings. Stacked self-attention blocks with
causal masking aggregate this sequence, so that each position attends only to
earlier interactions, and the output at the final position serves as the user's
sequence representation.

To rank a candidate item, SASRec takes the dot product between this sequence
representation and the candidate's item embedding, and orders candidates by the
resulting score. SASRec is trained on each dataset's interaction data and kept
frozen thereafter. It supplies the item embeddings that Stage~1 aligns with text,
the sequence representation used by both stages, and the collaborative score that
PopGate combines with the language model's.

Because item embeddings are learned solely from co-occurrence, the quality of this
collaborative signal varies across items. An item's embedding is updated only when
that item appears in a training sequence, so items observed many times acquire
embeddings that encode fine-grained behavioural structure, while items observed
rarely retain embeddings close to their initialisation and receive scores that carry
little information. Item popularity therefore governs how far the collaborative
signal can be trusted, a dependence that Section~\ref{sec:crossover-method}
quantifies and Section~\ref{sec:popgate} exploits.

\subsection{Stage 1: Sequential--Textual Alignment}
\label{sec:stage1}

Stage~1 constructs an item representation that preserves the sequential signal
learned from interaction data while incorporating information from textual
metadata. SASRec item embeddings encode collaborative relationships, but they
are tied to item identifiers and do not explicitly represent what an item is.
Textual metadata provides this complementary semantic description. The purpose
of the alignment stage is therefore to transfer textual information into the
collaborative representation without discarding its recommendation signal.

For each item, we pair its frozen 50-dimensional SASRec embedding with a
textual embedding. We encode the title and description using Sentence-BERT,
obtaining a 768-dimensional text embedding; the text encoder is fine-tuned
during alignment. Two encoder--decoder branches process the collaborative and
textual representations, respectively. Each
encoder maps its input into a shared 128-dimensional latent space, while the
corresponding decoder reconstructs the original input. The two inputs are thus
aligned through a common bottleneck rather than concatenated into a single
feature vector.

We optimise the alignment module with three complementary objectives. A
matching loss brings the collaborative and textual encodings closer in the
shared space. Reconstruction losses preserve the information carried by each
input. Finally, a binary
recommendation loss scores reconstructed positive and negative item embeddings
against the frozen SASRec sequence representation. This objective ensures that
the aligned representation retains the ranking structure learned from user--item
interactions, rather than optimising semantic agreement alone.

Stage~1 follows the alignment strategy of A-LLMRec~\cite{kim2024large}. Once
training is complete, only the collaborative-side encoder is retained. It maps
each SASRec item embedding to the 128-dimensional aligned item representation
used in Stage~2; the textual encoder and both reconstruction decoders are not
required at inference.


\subsection{Stage 2: LLM Integration}
\label{sec:stage2}

The goal of Stage~2 is to adapt the language model to the next-item recommendation
task. We use OPT-6.7B~\cite{opt} as the large language model. Two small projection
modules map the user and item representations into the model's token embedding space,
so that the collaborative signal reaches the language model without altering its
parameters.

Two non-textual signals are incorporated into the prompt. First, the \textbf{user
soft token} is the $50$-dimensional SASRec sequence embedding, which summarises the
user's whole historical preferences; a two-layer MLP projects it into the model's
$4096$-dimensional token space, and it is prepended as a single soft token at the
start of the input. Second, the \textbf{item soft tokens} at \texttt{[HistoryEmb]} and
\texttt{[CandidateEmb]} are the $128$-dimensional joint sequential--textual embeddings
from Stage~1, which preserve both ranking signal and item semantics, projected into
the same $4096$-dimensional token space by a second two-layer MLP.

These signals are assembled into a natural-language prompt. The prompt states the
titles of the ten most recent items in the user's history, instructs the model to
recommend the next item from a listed set of candidate titles. Each placeholder is positioned directly
after its item's title, so the language model observes the surface description of an
item and its collaborative representation at the same position.

Stage~2 trains only the two projection MLPs described above, while all other
components, the language model, SASRec, and the Stage-1 item encoder, remain frozen.
The model is trained for next-item prediction using a standard autoregressive
cross-entropy loss over the ground-truth next-item title, conditioned on the assembled
prompt; tokens belonging to the prompt itself are masked out of the loss.

At inference each candidate title is scored
by its length-normalised log-likelihood, the mean log-probability the model assigns to
the tokens of that title given the prompt, and candidates are ranked by this score.

\subsection{Measuring the Crossover}
\label{sec:crossover-method}

The collaborative recommender (Section~\ref{sec:cf}) and the language model
(Section~\ref{sec:stage2}) each assign every candidate a score, by dot product and by
title likelihood respectively. The goal here is to establish how the reliability of
these two scores varies with item popularity, and at which popularity one overtakes
the other. Both facts are prerequisites for PopGate (Section~\ref{sec:popgate}): the
first justifies gating on popularity at all, the second fixes where the gate should
hand over. We define the popularity of an item as the number of times it occurs in the
training split, a property of the item rather than of the user. Grouping test cases by the
popularity of their ground-truth item and comparing Hit@1 of the two scores within
each group yields an accuracy difference that varies with popularity; we call the
popularity at which this difference changes sign the \emph{crossover point}. To ask
whether that point is predictable from the data alone, we pair it with two descriptive
statistics of each dataset, the mean number of interactions per item and the mean user
history length, both computed over all interactions of the data.

\subsection{PopGate: Popularity-Gated Fusion}
\label{sec:popgate}

The collaborative score becomes more reliable as an item is observed more often in
training (Section~\ref{sec:cf}), whereas the language model reads a title that is
equally available at any popularity (Section~\ref{sec:stage2}). One score must
therefore overtake the other as popularity grows, and
Section~\ref{sec:crossover-method} measures where. A recommender that commits to a
single score gives up accuracy wherever the other is stronger. PopGate avoids the
choice. Rather than selecting a model per
dataset, it weights the two scores separately for each candidate, using that
candidate's own popularity as the weight.

Two conditions must hold before the scores can be combined. First, both must be
computed over the same candidate set: fusing rankings drawn from independently
sampled candidates would compare scores for items the other model never saw. We
therefore draw a single candidate set per user and score it with both the
collaborative recommender and the language model, retaining every candidate's score
rather than only the final ordering. Second, the two scores are not on a common
scale, one being a dot product and the other a mean log-probability. Within each
user we standardise each model's scores across its candidates to zero mean and unit
variance, giving comparable quantities $z_{\mathrm{CF}}$ and $z_{\mathrm{LLM}}$.

The gate itself is a single ratio. Writing $\mathrm{pop}(c)$ for candidate $c$'s
training popularity and $\pi$ for the mean popularity of the items appearing in the
training split,
\begin{equation}
w(c) = \frac{\mathrm{pop}(c)}{\mathrm{pop}(c) + \pi}, \qquad
s(c) = w(c)\, z_{\mathrm{CF}}(c) + \bigl(1 - w(c)\bigr) z_{\mathrm{LLM}}(c),
\label{eq:popgate}
\end{equation}
and candidates are ranked by $s$. A candidate far more popular than typical receives
$w \to 1$ and is judged almost entirely by the collaborative score; one far rarer
receives $w \to 0$ and is judged by the language model; one whose popularity matches
$\pi$ is judged by the two equally. The handover is smooth, and its midpoint sits
at the dataset's own scale rather than at a constant chosen by hand. Because
$\mathrm{pop}(c)$ and $\pi$ are both training statistics, the gate can be evaluated
for any candidate without knowing which is the target.

We compare PopGate against three alternatives that share its inputs.
\textbf{Reciprocal rank fusion} combines the two rankings by summing
$1/(60 + \text{rank})$, using only positions and discarding score magnitudes.
\textbf{Equal weighting} sets $w = 1/2$ for every candidate, fusing the two
standardised scores without reference to popularity. The third is an \textbf{oracle}
that routes the entire ranking to one model according to the true target item's
popularity; it is not deployable, since it presumes the answer, and serves only as an
upper bound on what routing by popularity can achieve.

Finally, to test whether the fixed form of the gate is what matters, we replace it
with a two-parameter learned alternative,
$w(c) = \sigma\bigl(a \log(\mathrm{pop}(c) + 1) + b\bigr)$, fitted on half the users
and evaluated on the other half.


\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}

We evaluate on four categories of the Amazon Review 2018 corpus~\cite{amazonreviews}:
Luxury Beauty, AMAZON\_FASHION, Toys \& Games, and Prime Pantry. Each provides user
purchase histories with timestamps together with item metadata. We use item titles and
descriptions to build the textual item representations of Section~\ref{sec:stage1},
and titles alone in the prompts of Section~\ref{sec:stage2}.

The four categories were chosen to span a wide range of interaction density, the
property that Section~\ref{sec:crossover-method} relates the crossover point to. Their
mean number of interactions per item ranges from $2.5$ to $19.2$, close to an
eightfold spread, and the proportion of rare target items varies correspondingly from
a quarter to three quarters.

Following common practice in sequential recommendation~\cite{sasrec}, we discard
sparse users and items before training, applying a four-review threshold to Luxury
Beauty and Toys \& Games and a five-review threshold to the other two. Each user's interactions
are then sorted chronologically to form the sequential task. Toys \& Games is far
larger than the other three, so we draw a random $15\%$ sample of its users under a
fixed seed, re-apply an iterative five-core filter,
and retrain SASRec on the sample. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} reports the resulting
statistics.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Following standard practice in sequential recommendation~\cite{sasrec}, we adopt the
leave-one-out evaluation strategy. For each user, the last item in the interaction
sequence is used for testing, the second-to-last item for validation, and the
remaining items for training. Users with fewer than three interactions cannot supply
all three parts and are excluded from evaluation. At test time the model is given the
training sequence extended with the validation item.

During evaluation, the ground-truth item is ranked against $19$ randomly sampled
negative items that the user has not interacted with, forming a candidate set of $20$
items per user. This size is fixed by the architecture rather than chosen: Stage~2 lists every candidate title in
the prompt, so prompt length grows with the candidate count, and OPT-6.7B accepts at
most $2{,}048$ positions.

We report Hit@$k$ for $k \in \{1, 5, 10\}$ and NDCG@$k$ for $k \in \{5, 10\}$.
Hit@$k$ is the proportion of test cases in which the ground-truth item appears among
the top $k$ ranked candidates. NDCG@$k$ reduces to $1/\log_2(r+1)$ when the ground-truth item
is ranked at position $r \le k$ and to zero otherwise, rewarding methods that place it
higher rather than merely within the cut-off.

Every metric is additionally reported on two slices of the test cases, split by the
popularity of the target item as defined in Section~\ref{sec:crossover-method}. A test
case is \emph{cold} if its target item occurs at most five times in the training split
and \emph{warm} otherwise. An average over all test cases conceals the difference
between the two scores, which is the effect this paper is concerned with. The
threshold itself is not load-bearing: the finer popularity binning of
Section~\ref{sec:crossover-method} shows the same effect across the whole popularity
range without depending on any single cut-off.



\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

We compare Pivot-Rec against four recommenders trained on interaction data alone:
\textbf{NCF}~\cite{he2017neural}, which learns the user--item interaction function
from implicit feedback and is the only non-sequential model in the set;
\textbf{GRU4Rec}~\cite{hidasi2015session}, recurrent; \textbf{NextItNet}
\cite{yuan2019simple}, dilated-convolutional; and \textbf{SASRec}~\cite{sasrec},
self-attentive. SASRec is also Pivot-Rec's collaborative component
(Section~\ref{sec:cf}), so that comparison isolates the contribution of the
language model and the gate rather than of a different backbone.

Two language-model recommenders follow. \textbf{A-LLMRec}~\cite{kim2024large}
projects collaborative representations into the token space of a frozen language
model and ranks candidates by that model's score alone, with no reference to how
often a candidate was observed in training. \textbf{LLM-only} prompts
OPT-6.7B~\cite{opt} zero-shot using the Stage-2 template of
Section~\ref{sec:stage2} with every learned signal removed --- no user
representation, no Stage-1 item embeddings, no soft tokens --- separating what the
language model contributes by reading titles from what the aligned collaborative
representations contribute. Three further comparisons, reciprocal rank fusion,
equal weighting and the popularity oracle, are variants of the gate rather than
independent recommenders and are defined alongside it in
Section~\ref{sec:popgate}. All baselines rank the same candidate sets under the
protocol of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.


\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:impl}

The SASRec backbone uses two self-attention blocks with a single head,
$50$-dimensional item embeddings, a maximum sequence length of $50$, and dropout
$0.5$. It is trained on each dataset's training split with Adam at a learning rate
of $10^{-3}$ for $200$ epochs and frozen thereafter. In Stage~1 the two
encoder--decoder pairs and the Sentence-BERT encoder
(\texttt{nq-distilbert-base-v1}) are trained for $10$ epochs with Adam at a
learning rate of $10^{-4}$ and a batch size of $32$; the item and text
reconstruction losses are weighted by $0.5$ and $0.2$, the matching and
recommendation losses by one. In Stage~2 only the user and item projection MLPs
are trained, again for $10$ epochs with Adam at a learning rate of $10^{-4}$;
OPT-6.7B is loaded in $8$-bit and none of its parameters are updated. All
experiments run on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 (48\,GB).
